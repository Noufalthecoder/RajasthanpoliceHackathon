Why CDR / IPDR investigation is slow, error-prone and hard to defend

Research behind SANCHAR-NETRA · Team HackCrew · compiled 21 Nov 2026 from government, court, standards and industry sources (numbered links on page 2)

1 The problem in one paragraph

Call Detail Records (CDR) and IP Detail Records (IPDR) are among the most common evidence sources in cyber-fraud and conventional cases, because every call, SMS and internet session leaves a record at the operator. The difficulty is not getting the data; it is turning it into verified, court-defensible leads quickly. Operators deliver different layouts, files run to thousands of rows, internet sessions hide behind shared public IPs, tower data only gives an approximate area, and since July 2024 electronic records need a formal certificate with a hash value to be admitted. Investigators still do much of this by hand[7][8], while reported cyber fraud keeps rising year on year.

3 How the evidence reaches the investigator

What exists

  • Retention: since Dec 2021 licensed operators must keep CDR, EDR and IPDR for at least two years (previously one)[4].
  • Request route: records are sought through the court or the police station in-charge under Section 94 BNSS (successor to Section 91 CrPC); the Rajasthan High Court has read telecom authorities into the related Section 95[9].
  • IPDR structure: DoT’s Nov 2021 IPDR format lists source IP and port, NAT-translated IP and port, destination IP and port, and start/end times[5].

What arrives in practice

  • Different formats per operator: times in AM/PM vs 24-hour, durations in seconds vs hh:mm:ss[6]; numbers with or without country code[7].
  • Spreadsheets with very large row counts, often hundreds of thousands (vendor description)[7].
  • Manual handling: a comparable Punjab (Pakistan) programme records that CDR analysis for major crimes was earlier done by hand in Excel, “time-consuming and prone to errors”[8]; Indian police are turning to AI for IPDR because manual review takes considerable time[10].

4 The core pain points (what an investigator actually hits)

Pain pointWhat the research shows
1Format chaos and volumeEvery operator’s file differs, so merging two suspects’ data means cleaning dates, durations, number formats and duplicates before any analysis is reliable[6][7]. Existing tools are described in an Indian academic paper as often expensive, expert-dependent and poorly adapted to regional data formats[11].
2Shared IPs hide the subscriberUnder carrier-grade NAT, one public IP is shared by many users for short periods, so attribution needs the source port, an exact timestamp and the operator’s NAT/AAA logs together[12]. Without the source port and exact time, law-enforcement progress is described as “highly unlikely”[13]. An IP alone is not an identity.
3Location is approximate, not a pinIn Rahil v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2025 INSC 858) the Supreme Court said tower data shows only which tower a phone connected to and gives an approximate area, not the exact site, called it hazardous to rely on it alone to prove presence, and noted no evidence had been led on the tower’s range[14]. Tools that draw a confident “suspect was here” dot invite exactly this challenge.

6 Limits of this research (stated plainly)

  • No independent Indian study quantifying investigation time lost to manual CDR analysis was found; the “manual, slow” point rests on a Pakistan case study, news reporting and vendor material, and should be presented as indicative, not measured.
  • Rajasthan 2024–25 figures come from news reports of police statements; national figures are from Parliament replies as reported in the press. Exact dates of the DoT amendments vary slightly between sources. Verify against the primary documents before quoting.
  • Legal points (BNSS, BSA, Rahil) are summarised from judgments and commentary for engineering context. They are not legal advice; the Section 94 BNSS procedure itself was not retrieved from statute text.
